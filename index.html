Fuel

Fuel your training with balanced meals, well-timed snacks, and plenty of water.

A footballer’s daily diet should include a variety of nourishing foods. The balance of foods can change depending on the timing and demands of training or competition.

Before a match: Aim to eat a meal about three to four hours beforehand, focusing on carbohydrates for energy and including some lean protein. Options include:

  • Chicken pasta
  • Rice with vegetables
  • A sandwich (with meat preferably)
  • Or any combination of the aforementioned items.

Before or during activity: A light, carbohydrate-rich snack can provide a convenient source of energy. Suitable options include:

  • Fruit bars
  • Energy bars
  • Honey

After a match or training session: A balanced snack or meal with carbohydrates and protein can support recovery. Options include:

  • Fruit smoothie
  • Yogurt with granola
  • Chicken wrap

On rest days, aim for balanced meals that include carbohydrate-rich foods such as rice, bread, or pasta, alongside protein sources such as chicken, fish, or other suitable options. Include a variety of nourishing foods and unsaturated fats, and limit foods high in trans fats. Individual nutrition needs vary, so seek advice from a qualified health professional when needed.

Mind

Strengthen focus and confidence through preparation, reflection, and consistent practice.

Reflecting on past achievements can build confidence and reinforce that mistakes do not define you. Instead, they can offer valuable opportunities to learn and improve.

Honest self-reflection can help you identify areas for growth. Stay focused on the present, avoid letting others’ opinions undermine your confidence, and remember that steady progress takes time.

Consistent, thoughtful preparation matters. The career of Brazilian striker Ronaldo Nazário, who overcame serious injuries, is a reminder of the importance of appropriate training, recovery, and care.

Finally, study professional players with a similar style of play. Observing their decisions and techniques can give you ideas to adapt to your own game.

Technical

Develop your first touch, passing, dribbling, and finishing through focused, regular practice.

Choose drills that match your position and development goals. The examples below can be adapted to suit your experience and training environment.

First touch and finishing: Set up a small square with four cones. Toss the ball into the air, control it inside the square, then turn and finish on goal. The drill can also help defenders practise controlling the ball under pressure.

Weaker-foot practice: Use juggling, stationary-ball exercises, and short touches to build comfort and control with your weaker foot. Developing both feet can make you a more versatile player.

Trident Turn Drill: Run between three cones, receive and return passes with one touch, then turn and dribble back. This sequence can practice acceleration, passing, concentration, and movement.

One-versus-one practice: An attacker receives the ball from a defender and attempts to get past them and score. This can develop decision-making in both attack and defence.

Goalkeeper reaction drill: Begin facing a goalpost, then turn quickly toward a partner and react to a shot. Use an appropriate pace and safe setup.

Body

Build strength, mobility, and fitness while prioritizing rest and recovery.

A footballer’s physique can vary with age, gender, individual build, and position. Young players are still growing, so their height and body composition may change over time. Some positions may favour particular physical attributes, but players of many different builds can succeed in every role.

Rather than treating height or body composition as strict requirements, focus on developing the qualities your position calls for. Goalkeepers and centre-backs may benefit from reach and strength, while full-backs and wingers often rely on speed and agility. Midfielders and forwards need a range of attributes depending on their role and style of play.

Football requires repeated bursts of high-intensity effort. Alongside general fitness, appropriately planned sprint and interval work can help prepare players for the demands of a match. For example, a coach may include short sprint intervals with recovery periods as part of a broader programme. Adapt the workload to your age, experience, and fitness level, and seek guidance from a qualified coach or health professional.

Strength training can include the lower legs, thighs and hamstrings, hip flexors, back, and core. A balanced programme can support performance and help reduce injury risk, but no exercise can guarantee injury prevention. Prioritize good technique, recovery, and a healthy approach to body composition.
